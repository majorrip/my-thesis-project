\chapter{Experimental Design and Results}
\label{chap:results}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Scope of this evaluation.} The results below were produced by the
accompanying \texttt{evaluate.py} on the three datasets described in
Section~\ref{sec:datasets}: a procedurally generated \emph{structured}
image set, the offline scikit-learn \emph{digits} set, and a subset of the
standard \emph{CIFAR-10} benchmark. The structured and digits runs were
executed in the primary environment (a network-restricted cloud CPU) and
independently reproduced on Google Colab; the CIFAR-10 run was performed on
Colab, where the benchmark could be downloaded. All figures are genuine
measurements of training dynamics and resource cost; no classification
accuracy is claimed, as the evaluation is at the level of the
self-supervised representation rather than a downstream classifier.
\end{quote}

\section{Research Questions}
\begin{description}
    \item[RQ1 (Collapse and placement):] Does the variance/covariance
    regularization prevent representational collapse relative to an
    unregularized ($\alpha = \beta = 0$) JEPA and converge stably, and does
    \emph{where} the two penalties are applied---on the context embedding
    $s_t$ or the prediction $s_{\text{pred}}$---govern whether collapse
    occurs?
    \item[RQ2 (Decentralization):] Does the corrected placement's collapse
    resistance persist when training is decentralized across federated,
    non-IID nodes?
    \item[RQ3 (Scale):] Do the placement effect and the rank--usefulness
    correspondence hold with a higher-capacity backbone (ResNet-18) at
    benchmark scale?
    \item[RQ4 (Efficiency):] What are the inference latency and memory
    footprint of EdgeVerify, and does the layer-wise local training scheme
    bound peak \emph{training} memory as encoder depth grows?
\end{description}

\section{Datasets}
\label{sec:datasets}
\paragraph{Structured.} Each $64\times64$ RGB image is generated with a
random background gradient and one to three random rectangles and circles,
yielding genuine local spatial structure (in contrast to the i.i.d.\ noise
used in the initial smoke test). The context view is produced by zeroing a
random rectangular region occupying $35$--$60\%$ of each side, and the
spatial action is the normalized bounding box of that region.

\paragraph{Digits.} The scikit-learn handwritten-digits dataset ($1797$
real $8\times8$ images) is upsampled to $64\times64$ and replicated to
three channels. It provides a small but \emph{real} data source available
without network access, used as a sanity check. The same random masking is
applied.

\paragraph{CIFAR-10.} A subset of the standard CIFAR-10 benchmark ($2000$
natural $32\times32$ images, upsampled to $64\times64$) is used to test
whether the findings on the smaller datasets transfer to real natural
images. It was run in the Colab environment where the benchmark could be
downloaded. The same random masking and spatial-action construction are
applied. A subset and a reduced epoch budget are used for tractability on
CPU; a full-dataset run is left to future work.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}
Two collapse indicators are computed over the context embedding
$s_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ ($d = 256$) on up to $512$ samples:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mean embedding std} $\bar\sigma = \frac{1}{d}\sum_j
    \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(s_{t,j})}$. Variance collapse drives
    $\bar\sigma \rightarrow 0$.
    \item \textbf{Effective rank} (participation ratio)
    $\big(\sum_i \lambda_i\big)^2 / \sum_i \lambda_i^2$, where $\lambda_i$
    are the eigenvalues of the embedding covariance. Dimensional collapse
    (correlated dimensions) drives this toward $1$.
\end{itemize}
Efficiency is reported as parameter count, parameter memory, and
single-sample inference latency (mean $\pm$ std over $50$ runs after
warm-up).

The results are mixed and warrant careful reading. The variance regularizer
is effective at its stated objective: across all three datasets the full
model maintains $\bar\sigma \approx 1.55$--$1.77$ (structured, digits) and
$1.68$ on CIFAR-10, at or above the hinge threshold $\gamma = 1.0$, whereas
the corresponding unregularized baselines collapse toward low per-dimension
variance ($\bar\sigma = 0.245$, $0.080$, and $0.223$). This directly
confirms that the variance term prevents the low-variance mode of collapse.

However, the \emph{effective rank} tells the opposite story: the full model
attains a very low effective rank ($1.64$, $1.03$, and $1.36$ out of $256$
for structured, digits, and CIFAR-10), and in every case it is \emph{lower}
than the corresponding unregularized baseline ($6.46$, $3.57$, and $4.53$).
High per-dimension variance therefore coexists with strongly correlated
dimensions---a rank-deficient representation. At the code's covariance
weight $\beta = 0.01$, the decorrelation term is too weak to counteract this,
which is consistent with the growing covariance component visible in
Figure~\ref{fig:losscurves}. \textbf{The regularization as configured
prevents variance collapse but does not, on this evidence, prevent
dimensional (rank) collapse.} Crucially, this pattern is reproduced on the
CIFAR-10 natural-image subset, indicating that it is not an artifact of the
synthetic or low-resolution data.

\subsection{Memory-Bounded Training: Depth Scaling (RQ4)}
\label{sec:memory}
The efficiency figures above concern inference. The training-time memory claim
of Chapter~\ref{chap:method}---that layer-wise local training bounds peak
memory as encoder depth grows, whereas end-to-end backpropagation does
not---is tested directly here. A depth-configurable convolutional encoder
($C = 128$ channels per block, $32 \times 32$ feature maps, batch size $128$)
is trained for $30$ steps under each scheme with the co-located
variance$+$covariance objective (the collapse-resistant placement of
Section~\ref{sec:placement}), and the real peak GPU memory
(\texttt{torch.cuda.max\_memory\_allocated}) is recorded on a Colab L4,
averaged over three seeds. Table~\ref{tab:memory} and Figure~\ref{fig:memory}
report the result.

    Local training holds peak memory near-constant while keeping effective rank
    well above the rank-$1$ collapse floor.}

Two observations follow. First, the memory scaling is as predicted and the
separation is large. End-to-end peak memory grows approximately linearly with
depth---from $862$~MB at depth $2$ to $4657$~MB at depth $32$ ($\approx 130$~MB
per added block)---while the layer-wise local scheme stays essentially flat,
rising only from $863$~MB to $938$~MB over the same range ($\approx 2.5$~MB per
block, a $9\%$ increase across a $16\times$ change in depth). At depth $32$ the
local scheme trains the same encoder in $938$~MB against $4657$~MB for
end-to-end---a $5.0\times$ reduction, or $3.7$~GB saved---which is the
difference between fitting and not fitting a deep encoder within a fixed
on-device budget. The common $\approx 862$~MB floor at depth $2$ is the CUDA
context, parameters, and optimizer state; the depth-dependent term above it is
the activation memory the scheme targets.

Second, the memory saving does not induce collapse. The effective rank of the
final embedding under local training stays in the range $3.0$--$3.6$ at every
depth---an order of magnitude above the rank-$1$ collapse floor---and is in fact
more stable than end-to-end, whose rank becomes noisy at large depth
($2.54 \pm 1.00$ at depth $32$ versus $3.29 \pm 0.10$ for local). Because the
inputs here are random tensors, these ranks are a \emph{non-collapse} check
rather than a downstream-quality measurement: they confirm that detaching the
graph between blocks does not drive the representation into a degenerate
subspace, not that the features are useful for a task.

Two caveats bound the result. The local scheme adds a lightweight per-block
head and performs one backward pass per block per step, trading a modest
increase in compute and parameter count for the memory reduction; and this is a
controlled micro-benchmark on synthetic inputs, designed to isolate the
memory--depth relationship rather than to train a downstream task. Within those
bounds, it establishes the thesis's low-power training claim as a \emph{measured}
effect: near-constant-memory training of deep encoders is achievable on
commodity hardware without representational collapse.

\subsection{Covariance-Weight Ablation}
\label{sec:beta}
The low effective rank of the full model raises the question of whether it is
simply a consequence of an under-weighted covariance term. To test this, the
covariance weight $\beta$ was swept over four orders of magnitude on the
structured dataset with the variance weight fixed ($\alpha = 1.0$);
Table~\ref{tab:beta} and Figure~\ref{fig:beta} report the outcome.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{Covariance-weight ($\beta$) ablation on structured data
    ($\alpha = 1.0$, single seed). Increasing $\beta$ raises effective rank
    monotonically, but by a small margin.}
    \label{tab:beta}
    \begin{tabular}{ccc}
        \toprule
        $\beta$ & $\bar\sigma$ & \textbf{Eff.\ rank} \\
        \midrule
        $0.01$ (default) & $1.551$ & $1.61$ \\
        $0.10$           & $1.567$ & $1.82$ \\
        $1.00$           & $1.567$ & $1.96$ \\
        $10.0$           & $1.471$ & $2.19$ \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{images/beta_ablation.png}
    \caption{Covariance-weight ablation. Effective rank increases
    monotonically with $\beta$ but remains far below the unregularized
    baseline ($\sim 6.5$, dashed) even at $\beta = 10$; mean embedding
    standard deviation is essentially unaffected.}
    \label{fig:beta}
\end{figure}

The ablation is informative in a negative sense. Increasing $\beta$ does move
effective rank in the expected direction---monotonically, from $1.61$ at the
default to $2.19$ at $\beta = 10$ (a $1000\times$ increase)---confirming that
the covariance term is directionally effective. However, the magnitude is
small: even at $\beta = 10$ the effective rank ($2.19$) remains far below both
the unregularized baseline ($\sim 6.5$) and the $256$-dimensional ceiling,
while $\bar\sigma$ stays essentially constant. Rank collapse is therefore
\emph{not} attributable merely to an under-weighted covariance penalty.
A more likely cause is structural: the variance and covariance penalties are
applied to \emph{different} tensors (the context embedding $s_t$ and the
prediction $s_{\text{pred}}$; Chapter~\ref{chap:method}), so the term that
sustains variance and the term that decorrelates never act on the same
representation. Testing a formulation that applies both penalties to a common
embedding is identified as the principal next step
(Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion}).

\subsection{Regularizer-Placement Study}
\label{sec:placement}
The $\beta$ ablation indicated that collapse is governed by \emph{where} the
penalties are applied, not by their weight. We test this systematically. Each
of the two penalties can act on either the context embedding $s_t$ or the
predictor output $s_{\text{pred}}$, giving a $2\times2$ placement grid, which
we evaluate at fixed weights ($\alpha = \beta = 1.0$) on both datasets. In all
cases the diagnostics are computed on the frozen context encoder---the
representation a downstream user consumes. Table~\ref{tab:placement} and
Figure~\ref{fig:placement} report the grid.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering

Three observations follow, and together they constitute the central finding of
this work. First, the collapse is \emph{specific}: of the four placements, only
one---variance on $s_t$ with covariance on $s_{\text{pred}}$, which is exactly
the original wiring---drives effective rank to $\approx 1$--$2$; every other
placement yields rank $\ge 4.7$ and, on digits, probe accuracy $\ge 0.92$.
Second, the harmful ingredient is identifiable: applying the variance
(scale) penalty to $s_t$ \emph{without} a co-located covariance
(decorrelation) penalty on $s_t$ inflates each dimension's magnitude while
leaving the dimensions free to correlate, producing a high-variance but
rank-deficient representation. Placing the covariance penalty on $s_t$
(whether or not the variance penalty is also there) avoids the collapse.
Third, and most cautionary, the failure is \emph{invisible to the objective}:
in the collapsed cell the final variance loss is exactly $0.000$---the VICReg
variance criterion is fully satisfied---and $\bar\sigma$ is the \emph{largest}
of any cell, yet the representation is rank-$1$ and least useful downstream.
Neither the training loss nor the variance criterion signals the problem; only
effective rank and the linear probe do.

We state the resulting design principle: \emph{in negative-free predictive SSL,
a variance penalty applied to an embedding must be paired with a covariance
penalty on the same embedding; a variance penalty without co-located
decorrelation is worse than no regularization at all.} This is not a
single-seed artifact. Table~\ref{tab:placement_ms} repeats the entire grid
over five seeds: the ordering is preserved in every case, with small variance.
The collapsed cell stays at rank $\approx 1$--$2$ while the healthy cells
remain an order of magnitude higher, and the separations far exceed the
standard deviations---the collapsed and healthy configurations do not overlap.

\subsection{Shared-Embedding Reformulation and Downstream Validation}
\label{sec:sharedfix}
Guided by the placement study, we adopt the best cell---both penalties on
$s_t$, the \emph{shared-embedding} variant (\texttt{shared\_cov=True},
$\alpha = \beta = 1.0$)---and validate it more thoroughly, extending the
downstream linear probe to CIFAR-10. The probe freezes the trained context
encoder, extracts features from the (unmasked) images, and fits a linear
classifier on the class labels. Probe accuracy is a downstream, ground-truth measure of representation
usefulness that the intrinsic collapse metrics lack. Structured data carries
no labels, so the probe is reported for the labeled digits and CIFAR-10 sets.

The result is instructive on three points. First, the structural diagnosis is
confirmed on all three datasets: the shared-embedding variant raises effective
rank from $1.64$ to $47.44$ (structured), $1.03$ to $34.15$ (digits), and
$1.34$ to $39.24$ (CIFAR-10)---an improvement more than an order of magnitude
larger than anything the $\beta$ sweep achieved, and one that holds on real
natural images. Second, the linear probe shows the rank collapse was not
benign: on \emph{both} probes the original configuration is the worst
(accuracy $0.561$ on digits and $0.313$ on CIFAR-10), below both the
unregularized baseline ($0.944$; $0.415$) and the shared-embedding variant
($0.937$; $0.405$). In other words, the regularizer as originally wired
\emph{harmed} the representation relative to using no regularization at all,
and the shared-embedding reformulation repairs this on both datasets. It is
important to be precise about the magnitude of that repair: the fix restores
accuracy to \emph{parity} with the unregularized baseline, not above it, so
the contribution is the removal of a harmful failure mode rather than a net
gain over no regularization. Third, the probe validates the choice of
diagnostic: the original model has the largest $\bar\sigma$ yet the lowest
accuracy, whereas accuracy tracks effective rank---confirming that effective
rank, not embedding standard deviation or loss magnitude, is the metric
aligned with representation quality here.

Several caveats apply. These runs use a single seed. Absolute CIFAR-10
accuracy is low ($\sim 0.4$) because of the deliberately small setup
(lightweight encoder, $2000$-image subset, $15$ epochs), so the CIFAR-10 probe
should be read as a relative comparison, not an accuracy benchmark. The
shared-embedding variant also lowers $\bar\sigma$ (energy is spread across many
more dimensions), so the variance hinge is no longer saturated; the probe
indicates this does not harm usefulness, but a joint tuning of $\alpha$ and
$\beta$ under the shared formulation is a natural follow-up.

\subsection{Decentralized (Multi-Agent) Training (RQ2)}
\label{sec:federated}
The edge and Dew setting that motivates EdgeVerify is inherently
decentralized, so a natural question is whether the placement finding and the
shared-embedding fix survive when training is distributed across agents rather
than centralized. We test this with a minimal two-node federated scheme. The
data are split into two disjoint shards; each node trains locally on its shard
for several epochs; after each communication round the nodes' online
parameters (context encoder, action encoder, and predictor, including
BatchNorm statistics) are averaged (FedAvg) into a global model and broadcast
back, and the target encoders are re-synchronised to the global context
encoder. We run $6$ rounds (local epochs $6$/$4$ for structured/digits) and
compare three settings: centralized shared-embedding training (reference),
federated shared-embedding, and federated with the original wiring.
Table~\ref{tab:federated} and Figure~\ref{fig:federated} report the outcome.

Two conclusions follow. First, the fix is \emph{compatible with
decentralization}: federated shared-embedding training preserves high
effective rank ($26.89$ structured, $31.01$ digits) and downstream accuracy
($0.941$ on digits, statistically indistinguishable from the centralized
$0.934$), despite parameter averaging across nodes trained on disjoint data.
Second, the collapse is a property of the \emph{objective}, not the training
regime: the original wiring collapses under federation exactly as it does
centrally (rank $2.69$/$1.05$, probe $0.628$). Correct regularizer placement is
therefore what enables collapse-resistant self-supervised learning on
decentralized edge nodes---directly connecting the design principle of
Section~\ref{sec:placement} to the deployment setting the architecture targets.
We further stress the decentralized setting with more clients and
\emph{non-IID} data. Using Dirichlet($\alpha$) label-skewed partitions (smaller
$\alpha$ = stronger skew) with size-weighted FedAvg, Table~\ref{tab:noniid} and
Figure~\ref{fig:noniid} report the shared and original placements across

throughout---effective rank $22$--$27$ and probe accuracy $0.93$--$0.95$---even
at $10$ clients with strong label skew ($\alpha = 0.1$), while the original
wiring collapses to rank $1.01$ and probe $\approx 0.66$ in every setting.
Correct placement is thus robust to the data heterogeneity that characterizes
realistic federated deployment. Communication-efficiency trade-offs and
heterogeneous or asynchronous clients remain for future work.

\subsection{Scaling Up: ResNet-18 on Full CIFAR-10 (RQ3)}
\label{sec:scale}
The experiments so far use the lightweight CNN. To test whether the findings
hold with a standard backbone at benchmark scale, we replace the encoder with a
\textbf{ResNet-18} and train on all $50{,}000$ CIFAR-10 training images,
evaluating each placement with a linear probe on the $10{,}000$-image test set
(single seed, $20$ epochs). Table~\ref{tab:scale} and Figure~\ref{fig:scale}
report the outcome, and it \emph{revises} the small-scale picture in two
important ways.

First, the collapse is far milder with a high-capacity backbone. The original
wiring reaches effective rank $9.9$ rather than the $\approx 1$ seen with the
CNN; the catastrophic rank-1 collapse is therefore, in large part, a property
of the \emph{small model} rather than of the objective alone. The placement
still orders the configurations---the original wiring is the lowest-rank
regularized setting and the shared variant the highest ($229$ of $256$)---so
the mechanism persists, but its severity does not transfer.

Second, and more consequentially, \emph{effective rank ceases to predict
downstream usefulness at this scale}. The shared placement attains by far the
highest rank yet the \emph{lowest} probe accuracy ($0.358$), and no regularized
configuration exceeds the unregularized baseline ($0.438$); the best downstream
accuracy comes from the unregularized and both-$s_{\text{pred}}$ settings
($\approx 0.44$). The correspondence between effective rank and linear-probe
accuracy observed on the small datasets (Section~\ref{sec:placement}) thus does
\emph{not} generalize to a ResNet-18 on natural images: maximizing rank via the
shared reformulation does not improve---and slightly harms---the learned
representation's usefulness.

These results are reported plainly because they qualify the thesis's central
claims rather than confirm them. The placement effect on \emph{rank} is real
and reproduces across scales, but (i) its extreme form is a small-model
artifact, and (ii) the link from rank to downstream quality---and hence the
practical value of the shared reformulation---is scale-dependent and does not
hold at ResNet/CIFAR scale. Two caveats temper even this negative result: the
run is single-seed, and the self-supervised training is short ($20$ epochs,
absolute probe accuracy $\approx 0.44$), so the representations are weak in
absolute terms. A longer, multi-seed study is needed to characterize the scale
behavior fully; nonetheless the direction is clear enough to caution against
treating effective rank as a proxy for representation quality at scale.

\section{Discussion}
On RQ1, the evidence is partial: the variance constraint demonstrably
mitigates variance collapse, but at $\beta = 0.01$ the covariance constraint
does not yield a high-rank representation, and the unregularized baseline is
paradoxically higher-rank---a pattern that holds on the CIFAR-10 natural-image
subset as well as on the smaller datasets. A plausible explanation is that the variance and
covariance penalties act on different tensors (the context embedding $s_t$
and the prediction $s_{\text{pred}}$, respectively; Chapter~\ref{chap:method})
and that the covariance weight is small relative to the variance weight. The
covariance-weight ablation (Section~\ref{sec:beta}) tests this directly and
shows that increasing $\beta$ alone is insufficient to restore rank, which
points to the placement of the penalties rather than their weight. The
placement study (Section~\ref{sec:placement}) makes this precise: collapse is
unique to variance-on-$s_t$ without a co-located covariance term, and it is
invisible to both the loss and the variance criterion. Acting on that cause,
the shared-embedding reformulation (Section~\ref{sec:sharedfix}) restores
effective rank by more than an order of magnitude and, as the linear probe
confirms, recovers downstream accuracy to the level of the unregularized
baseline; Section~\ref{sec:federated} further shows the correction survives
decentralized training. A crucial qualification follows from the scaling study
(Section~\ref{sec:scale}): the alignment between effective rank and downstream
usefulness that underpins this diagnosis holds at small scale but \emph{breaks}
for a ResNet-18 on CIFAR-10, where the highest-rank configuration is the least
useful and no regularizer beats the unregularized baseline. The placement
effect on rank persists across scales; its practical benefit does not. On
RQ2 (decentralization), the corrected placement's collapse resistance
transfers to federated training and survives non-IID label skew with up to
ten clients (Section~\ref{sec:federated}), whereas the original wiring
collapses in every regime; on RQ3 (scale), the placement effect on rank
reproduces with a ResNet-18 but its practical benefit does not, as detailed
above. On RQ4, the architecture is genuinely lightweight ($2.84$~MB of
parameters, millisecond CPU inference), supporting its suitability for
edge deployment in terms of size. Finally, on the convergence sub-question
of RQ1, the distillation term converges rapidly in all cases; the full
objective's total loss is non-monotonic because the covariance term grows
once distillation is satisfied.

Notably, the unregularized baseline attains a far lower total loss
($\sim 10^{-3}$) precisely because, with $\alpha = \beta = 0$, the objective
reduces to distillation MSE, which is trivially minimized while the
representation quietly loses variance. This is a concrete illustration of
why loss magnitude alone is an inadequate proxy for representation quality,
and why the collapse indicators are necessary.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}
The following factors were examined and, where possible, addressed.
Run-to-run variance, initially a concern, has been \emph{quantified} across
the study: the original collapse (five seeds, Section~\ref{sec:multiseed}), the
full placement grid (five seeds, Table~\ref{tab:placement_ms}), and the
federated experiment (three seeds, Table~\ref{tab:federated}) are all stable,
with the key separations far exceeding their standard deviations. The main
residual single-seed result is the CIFAR-10 probe.
Representation \emph{usefulness}, previously unmeasured, is now assessed by a
linear probe on the labeled digits and CIFAR-10 sets (Section~\ref{sec:sharedfix}).
Three factors remain open. First, scale: the study spans a lightweight CNN and
a ResNet-18 on full CIFAR-10 (Section~\ref{sec:scale}), but the ResNet run is
single-seed and short ($20$ epochs), and---most importantly---it shows that the
practical benefit of the reformulation does not survive at that scale, so the
scale behavior is not yet settled and requires longer, multi-seed training.
Second, evaluation depth: representation usefulness is measured only by a linear
probe; a stronger downstream protocol may read the scaled representations
differently. Third, all measurements are CPU-only, so latency and memory would
differ on target embedded hardware. Addressing these is the subject of
Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion}.
