\chapter{Experimental Design and Results}
\label{chap:results}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Scope of this evaluation.} The results below were produced by the
accompanying \texttt{evaluate.py} on the three datasets described in
Section~\ref{sec:datasets}: a procedurally generated \emph{structured}
image set, the offline scikit-learn \emph{digits} set, and a subset of the
standard \emph{CIFAR-10} benchmark. The structured and digits runs were
executed in the primary environment (a network-restricted cloud CPU) and
independently reproduced on Google Colab; the CIFAR-10 run was performed on
Colab, where the benchmark could be downloaded. All figures are genuine
measurements of training dynamics and resource cost; no classification
accuracy is claimed, as the evaluation is at the level of the
self-supervised representation rather than a downstream classifier.
\end{quote}

\section{Research Questions}
\begin{description}
    \item[RQ1 (Collapse):] Does the variance/covariance regularization
    prevent representational collapse relative to an unregularized
    ($\alpha = \beta = 0$) JEPA?
    \item[RQ2 (Efficiency):] What are the inference latency and memory
    footprint of EdgeVerify?
    \item[RQ3 (Convergence):] Does the local objective converge stably?
\end{description}

\section{Experimental Setup}
Experiments were run on CPU using PyTorch (version~2.14 in the primary cloud
environment; the Colab reproduction used its default runtime). The
architecture and optimizer follow Chapter~\ref{chap:method} exactly
(Table~\ref{tab:hparams}). The primary runs use a fixed seed
(\texttt{seed}$=0$); a five-seed robustness study quantifying run-to-run
variance is reported in Section~\ref{sec:multiseed}. The structured and
digits runs were additionally reproduced on an independent machine (Google Colab) to check reproducibility
(Section~\ref{sec:repro}). Table~\ref{tab:setup} lists the run configuration.

\section{Datasets}
\label{sec:datasets}
\paragraph{Structured.} Each $64\times64$ RGB image is generated with a
random background gradient and one to three random rectangles and circles,
yielding genuine local spatial structure (in contrast to the i.i.d.\ noise
used in the initial smoke test). The context view is produced by zeroing a
random rectangular region occupying $35$--$60\%$ of each side, and the
spatial action is the normalized bounding box of that region.

\paragraph{Digits.} The scikit-learn handwritten-digits dataset ($1797$
real $8\times8$ images) is upsampled to $64\times64$ and replicated to
three channels. It provides a small but \emph{real} data source available
without network access, used as a sanity check. The same random masking is
applied.

\paragraph{CIFAR-10.} A subset of the standard CIFAR-10 benchmark ($2000$
natural $32\times32$ images, upsampled to $64\times64$) is used to test
whether the findings on the smaller datasets transfer to real natural
images. It was run in the Colab environment where the benchmark could be
downloaded. The same random masking and spatial-action construction are
applied. A subset and a reduced epoch budget are used for tractability on
CPU; a full-dataset run is left to future work.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}
Two collapse indicators are computed over the context embedding
$s_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ ($d = 256$) on up to $512$ samples:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mean embedding std} $\bar\sigma = \frac{1}{d}\sum_j
    \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(s_{t,j})}$. Variance collapse drives
    $\bar\sigma \rightarrow 0$.
    \item \textbf{Effective rank} (participation ratio)
    $\big(\sum_i \lambda_i\big)^2 / \sum_i \lambda_i^2$, where $\lambda_i$
    are the eigenvalues of the embedding covariance. Dimensional collapse
    (correlated dimensions) drives this toward $1$.
\end{itemize}
Efficiency is reported as parameter count, parameter memory, and
single-sample inference latency (mean $\pm$ std over $50$ runs after
warm-up).

The results are mixed and warrant careful reading. The variance regularizer
is effective at its stated objective: across all three datasets the full
model maintains $\bar\sigma \approx 1.55$--$1.77$ (structured, digits) and
$1.68$ on CIFAR-10, at or above the hinge threshold $\gamma = 1.0$, whereas
the corresponding unregularized baselines collapse toward low per-dimension
variance ($\bar\sigma = 0.245$, $0.080$, and $0.223$). This directly
confirms that the variance term prevents the low-variance mode of collapse.

However, the \emph{effective rank} tells the opposite story: the full model
attains a very low effective rank ($1.64$, $1.03$, and $1.36$ out of $256$
for structured, digits, and CIFAR-10), and in every case it is \emph{lower}
than the corresponding unregularized baseline ($6.46$, $3.57$, and $4.53$).
High per-dimension variance therefore coexists with strongly correlated
dimensions---a rank-deficient representation. At the code's covariance
weight $\beta = 0.01$, the decorrelation term is too weak to counteract this,
which is consistent with the growing covariance component visible in
Figure~\ref{fig:losscurves}. \textbf{The regularization as configured
prevents variance collapse but does not, on this evidence, prevent
dimensional (rank) collapse.} Crucially, this pattern is reproduced on the
CIFAR-10 natural-image subset, indicating that it is not an artifact of the
synthetic or low-resolution data.

\subsection{Robustness Across Seeds}
\label{sec:multiseed}

experiments were repeated over five random seeds (data content held fixed;

\subsection{Covariance-Weight Ablation}
\label{sec:beta}
The low effective rank of the full model raises the question of whether it is
simply a consequence of an under-weighted covariance term. To test this, the
covariance weight $\beta$ was swept over four orders of magnitude on the
structured dataset with the variance weight fixed ($\alpha = 1.0$);
Table~\ref{tab:beta} and Figure~\ref{fig:beta} report the outcome.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{Covariance-weight ($\beta$) ablation on structured data
    ($\alpha = 1.0$, single seed). Increasing $\beta$ raises effective rank
    monotonically, but by a small margin.}
    \label{tab:beta}
    \begin{tabular}{ccc}
        \toprule
        $\beta$ & $\bar\sigma$ & \textbf{Eff.\ rank} \\
        \midrule
        $0.01$ (default) & $1.551$ & $1.61$ \\
        $0.10$           & $1.567$ & $1.82$ \\
        $1.00$           & $1.567$ & $1.96$ \\
        $10.0$           & $1.471$ & $2.19$ \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{images/beta_ablation.png}
    \caption{Covariance-weight ablation. Effective rank increases
    monotonically with $\beta$ but remains far below the unregularized
    baseline ($\sim 6.5$, dashed) even at $\beta = 10$; mean embedding
    standard deviation is essentially unaffected.}
    \label{fig:beta}
\end{figure}

The ablation is informative in a negative sense. Increasing $\beta$ does move
effective rank in the expected direction---monotonically, from $1.61$ at the
default to $2.19$ at $\beta = 10$ (a $1000\times$ increase)---confirming that
the covariance term is directionally effective. However, the magnitude is
small: even at $\beta = 10$ the effective rank ($2.19$) remains far below both
the unregularized baseline ($\sim 6.5$) and the $256$-dimensional ceiling,
while $\bar\sigma$ stays essentially constant. Rank collapse is therefore
\emph{not} attributable merely to an under-weighted covariance penalty.
A more likely cause is structural: the variance and covariance penalties are
applied to \emph{different} tensors (the context embedding $s_t$ and the
prediction $s_{\text{pred}}$; Chapter~\ref{chap:method}), so the term that
sustains variance and the term that decorrelates never act on the same
representation. Testing a formulation that applies both penalties to a common
embedding is identified as the principal next step
(Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion}).

\section{Discussion}
On RQ1, the evidence is partial: the variance constraint demonstrably
mitigates variance collapse, but at $\beta = 0.01$ the covariance constraint
does not yield a high-rank representation, and the unregularized baseline is
paradoxically higher-rank---a pattern that holds on the CIFAR-10 natural-image
subset as well as on the smaller datasets. A plausible explanation is that the variance and
covariance penalties act on different tensors (the context embedding $s_t$
and the prediction $s_{\text{pred}}$, respectively; Chapter~\ref{chap:method})
and that the covariance weight is small relative to the variance weight. The
covariance-weight ablation (Section~\ref{sec:beta}) tests this directly and
shows that increasing $\beta$ alone is insufficient to restore rank, which
points to the different-tensors structure as the underlying cause rather than
mere mis-weighting. On RQ2, the architecture is genuinely lightweight ($2.84$~MB of
parameters, millisecond CPU inference), supporting its suitability for
edge deployment in terms of size. On RQ3, the distillation term converges
rapidly in all cases; the full objective's total loss is non-monotonic
because the covariance term grows once distillation is satisfied.

Notably, the unregularized baseline attains a far lower total loss
($\sim 10^{-3}$) precisely because, with $\alpha = \beta = 0$, the objective
reduces to distillation MSE, which is trivially minimized while the
representation quietly loses variance. This is a concrete illustration of
why loss magnitude alone is an inadequate proxy for representation quality,
and why the collapse indicators are necessary.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}
The following factors were examined and, where possible, addressed.
Run-to-run variance, initially a concern, has been \emph{quantified}: the
five-seed study (Section~\ref{sec:multiseed}) shows the finding is stable and
the full-vs-unregularized separation holds for every seed. Two factors remain
open. First, the benchmark coverage is partial: two of the three datasets are
non-standard, and the CIFAR-10 evaluation used a $2000$-image subset with a
reduced epoch budget rather than the full benchmark. Second, all measurements
are CPU-only, so latency and memory would differ on target embedded hardware,
and the encoder is small relative to the transformer backbones typical of the
JEPA literature. Addressing these is the subject of
Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion}.
